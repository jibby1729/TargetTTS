\section{Introduction}

Modern speech-to-text systems perform well on clean, single-speaker audio but degrade sharply when speakers overlap~\citep{Chime, overlap}. This is a common occurrence in meetings, court proceedings, and media such as films or talk shows. Rather than attempting full diarization and separation, target-speaker transcription offers a more focused alternative: given a short enrollment clip, transcribe only the speaker of interest.

Additionally, the rise of full-duplex speech-to-speech models is increasing the demand for labelled transcriptions of natural conversations with interruptions~\citep{Moshi, PersonaPlex}. A reliable target-speaker system could automatically generate such labels by isolating each participant and producing timed transcripts, even through overlapping speech.

The objective of this project is to develop a target-speaker speech-to-text system that takes (i) a short enrollment utterance of the target speaker and (ii) a multi-speaker mixture, and outputs only the target speaker's transcript.


\section{Problem Formulation}

Let $\mathbf{x}_{\text{mix}} \in \mathbb{R}^T$ be a single-channel audio mixture containing overlapping speech from $N \geq 2$ speakers. We are also given a short enrollment utterance $\mathbf{x}_{\text{enroll}} \in \mathbb{R}^{T_e}$ of the target speaker $s^*$. The goal is to produce a transcript $\hat{y}$ that matches the ground-truth transcript $y^*$ of speaker $s^*$ within the mixture, as measured by word error rate:
\[
\text{WER} = \frac{S + D + I}{N_{\text{ref}}}
\]
where $S$, $D$, $I$ are the number of substitutions, deletions, and insertions respectively, and $N_{\text{ref}}$ is the number of words in the reference transcript.

A secondary evaluation criterion is non-target leakage: the rate at which words from interfering speakers appear in the output transcript.


\section{Proposed Design}

Our pipeline consists of three stages:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Speaker embedding extraction.} A pretrained speaker verification model encodes the enrollment utterance $\mathbf{x}_{\text{enroll}}$ into a fixed-dimensional speaker embedding $\mathbf{e} \in \mathbb{R}^d$.

    \item \textbf{Speaker-conditioned extraction.} A lightweight neural module receives the mixture $\mathbf{x}_{\text{mix}}$ (or its spectrogram) together with the speaker embedding $\mathbf{e}$ and produces either (a) a time--frequency mask that isolates the target speaker's signal, or (b) a conditioned representation that is passed directly to the ASR encoder. This module is the only component that is trained; the speaker encoder and ASR model remain frozen.

    \item \textbf{ASR decoding.} A pretrained ASR model transcribes the extracted or conditioned signal into text. We apply text normalization to both predicted and reference transcripts before computing WER, ensuring fair comparison.
\end{enumerate}

For baselines, we run the ASR model directly on (a) clean single-speaker audio and (b) raw mixtures without any conditioning, to quantify the WER degradation caused by overlap and the improvement provided by speaker conditioning.

This normalization is applied to both ground-truth and ASR output before WER computation.

\section{Speaker-Conditioned Extraction Network}

This section describes the architecture of the speaker-conditioned extraction module in detail.
The module is the only trained component in our pipeline; the speaker encoder (ECAPA-TDNN) and the ASR decoder (Whisper) remain frozen.

\subsection{Speaker embedding: ECAPA-TDNN}

The first stage of the pipeline converts a short enrollment utterance $\mathbf{x}_{\text{enroll}} \in \mathbb{R}^{T_e}$ into a fixed-dimensional speaker embedding $\mathbf{e} \in \mathbb{R}^{192}$. We use a pretrained ECAPA-TDNN~\citep{ECAPA} (\texttt{speechbrain/spkrec-ecapa-voxceleb}), kept frozen throughout training.

\subsubsection{Input representation}
The raw waveform is first converted to 80-dimensional log Mel-filterbank energies (MFBEs), computed with a 25\,ms window and 10\,ms hop (the same framing as our STFT). This yields a matrix $\mathbf{F} \in \mathbb{R}^{80 \times T_f}$ where $T_f$ is the number of frames. Unlike the extraction network, which operates on the full linear-frequency spectrogram to enable inversion, the speaker encoder uses the Mel scale because speaker identity is captured well by the lower-frequency spectral envelope.

\subsubsection{Architecture}

ECAPA-TDNN extends the classical TDNN (Time-Delay Neural Network) with three key innovations. Each component is described below and illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:ecapa-arch}.

\paragraph{(1) SE-Res2Net blocks.}
The core of the network is a stack of three \emph{SE-Res2Net} blocks~\citep{Res2Net}. Within each block, the channels of the 1D-convolutional layer are split into $s$ groups that are processed hierarchically: group $g$ receives the sum of its own input and the output of group $g{-}1$, enabling multi-scale temporal feature extraction at different receptive fields within a single layer. After the convolution, a Squeeze-Excitation (SE) module~\citep{SE} recalibrates channel responses:
\begin{align}
    \mathbf{s} &= \sigma\!\bigl(\mathbf{W}_2\, \text{ReLU}(\mathbf{W}_1\, \bar{\mathbf{h}})\bigr) \in \mathbb{R}^{C} \\
    \hat{\mathbf{h}}_t &= \mathbf{s} \odot \mathbf{h}_t
\end{align}
where $\bar{\mathbf{h}} = \frac{1}{T_f}\sum_t \mathbf{h}_t$ is the channel-wise mean over time, $\mathbf{W}_1 \in \mathbb{R}^{C/r \times C}$ and $\mathbf{W}_2 \in \mathbb{R}^{C \times C/r}$ are bottleneck projection matrices (reduction ratio $r = 4$), and $\sigma$ is the sigmoid function. This lets the network learn which channels are most informative for distinguishing speakers and suppress irrelevant ones.

Each SE-Res2Net block uses a 1D convolution with dilation factors $d \in \{2, 3, 4\}$ respectively, and all blocks use residual connections.

\paragraph{(2) Multi-layer feature aggregation (MFA).}
Rather than using only the output of the final block, ECAPA-TDNN concatenates the outputs of \emph{all three} SE-Res2Net blocks along the channel dimension. A $1 \times 1$ convolution then fuses them:
\[
    \mathbf{G} = \text{Conv1D}_{1\times1}\!\bigl([\mathbf{H}^{(1)} ; \mathbf{H}^{(2)} ; \mathbf{H}^{(3)}]\bigr) \in \mathbb{R}^{C' \times T_f}
\]
This aggregation ensures that both low-level (e.g.\ pitch, formant structure) and high-level (e.g.\ speaking style) features contribute to the final embedding.

\paragraph{(3) Attentive statistical pooling.}
Variable-length frame sequences must be collapsed to a fixed-size vector. ECAPA-TDNN uses attention-weighted statistics. An attention network computes a scalar weight $\alpha_t$ for each frame:
\[
    \alpha_t = \frac{\exp\!\bigl(\mathbf{v}^{\top} f(\mathbf{W} \mathbf{G}_{:,t} + \mathbf{b})\bigr)}
               {\sum_{t'} \exp\!\bigl(\mathbf{v}^{\top} f(\mathbf{W} \mathbf{G}_{:,t'} + \mathbf{b})\bigr)}
\]
The weighted mean and weighted standard deviation are computed and concatenated:
\begin{align}
    \boldsymbol{\mu} &= \sum_t \alpha_t\, \mathbf{G}_{:,t}, &
    \boldsymbol{\sigma} &= \sqrt{\sum_t \alpha_t\, (\mathbf{G}_{:,t} - \boldsymbol{\mu})^2} \\
    \mathbf{p} &= [\boldsymbol{\mu} ; \boldsymbol{\sigma}] \in \mathbb{R}^{2C'}
\end{align}
This is superior to simple mean pooling because the attention mechanism learns to emphasize frames that are most discriminative for speaker identity (e.g.\ voiced segments with clear harmonic structure) and down-weight silence or noise.

\paragraph{Output.}
A final fully connected layer projects the pooled statistics to the 192-dimensional embedding:
\[
    \mathbf{e} = \text{BN}(\mathbf{W}_{\text{out}}\, \mathbf{p} + \mathbf{b}_{\text{out}}) \in \mathbb{R}^{192}
\]
During pretraining (on VoxCeleb1+2), an AAM-softmax classification head was used. We discard this head and use only the 192-dim embedding vector, which serves as a compact speaker fingerprint.

\subsection{Extraction network overview}

The extraction network receives two inputs:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item The magnitude spectrogram of the mixture, $|\mathbf{X}_{\text{mix}}| \in \mathbb{R}^{F \times T}$, where $F$ is the number of frequency bins and $T$ is the number of time frames.
    \item A speaker embedding $\mathbf{e} \in \mathbb{R}^{d}$ extracted from a short enrollment utterance of the target speaker by a frozen ECAPA-TDNN model ($d = 192$).
\end{enumerate}

The output is a soft mask $\mathbf{M} \in [0,1]^{F \times T}$. The estimated target spectrogram is obtained by element-wise multiplication (the $\odot$ operator multiplies each time-frequency bin independently):
\[
    |\hat{\mathbf{X}}_{\text{target}}|(f,t) = \mathbf{M}(f,t) \cdot |\mathbf{X}_{\text{mix}}|(f,t), \qquad \forall\; f \in \{1,\dots,F\},\; t \in \{1,\dots,T\}
\]
or equivalently in matrix notation: $|\hat{\mathbf{X}}_{\text{target}}| = \mathbf{M} \odot |\mathbf{X}_{\text{mix}}|$.
The phase of the original mixture is reused to reconstruct the time-domain waveform via the inverse STFT:
\[
    \hat{\mathbf{x}}_{\text{target}} = \text{iSTFT}\!\bigl(|\hat{\mathbf{X}}_{\text{target}}| \cdot e^{j\angle \mathbf{X}_{\text{mix}}}\bigr)
\]

\subsubsection{STFT parameters and the meaning of $F$ and $T$}

A raw audio waveform is a one-dimensional sequence of amplitude values over time. While this representation captures all information, it does not directly reveal which frequencies are active at each moment. The Short-Time Fourier Transform (STFT) addresses this by sliding a short window across the waveform and computing a discrete Fourier transform within each window. The result is a two-dimensional complex-valued matrix $\mathbf{X} \in \mathbb{C}^{F \times T}$, where each entry $\mathbf{X}(f, t)$ encodes the amplitude and phase of frequency bin $f$ during time frame $t$. Taking the absolute value yields the \emph{magnitude spectrogram} $|\mathbf{X}| \in \mathbb{R}^{F \times T}$, which tells us \emph{how much} energy is present at each frequency and time, and is the representation our mask network operates on.

Crucially, the magnitude spectrogram is \emph{not} a log-mel spectrogram (which is lossy and non-invertible). It retains the full linear frequency resolution, which we need precisely because we must invert back to a waveform after masking via the inverse STFT (iSTFT).

We use the following STFT parameters, which are standard for 16\,kHz speech processing:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Window length:} 25\,ms $= 400$ samples. Each frame captures a 25\,ms snapshot of the signal, multiplied by a Hann window.
    \item \textbf{Hop length:} 10\,ms $= 160$ samples. Adjacent frames overlap by 15\,ms and are spaced 10\,ms apart.
    \item \textbf{FFT size:} $N_{\text{fft}} = 512$. Zero-padded from the 400-sample window.
    \item \textbf{Frequency bins:} $F = N_{\text{fft}}/2 + 1 = 257$. These are linearly spaced from 0\,Hz to the Nyquist frequency (8000\,Hz), each covering $\approx$31\,Hz.
    \item \textbf{Time frames:} $T = \lfloor (\text{signal length} - \text{window}) / \text{hop} \rfloor + 1$. A 5-second utterance gives $T \approx 500$ frames.
\end{itemize}

Each column $\mathbf{X}_{:,t} \in \mathbb{R}^F$ is a vector of $F = 257$ magnitude values representing the frequency content of the 25\,ms window centred at time $t \times 10\,\text{ms}$. Row $f$ across all columns traces how the energy at frequency $f \times 31\,\text{Hz}$ evolves over time.

\subsection{Architecture}

The network follows the VoiceFilter design~\citep{VoiceFilter} and consists of three stages: a convolutional encoder, a recurrent sequence model, and a fully connected mask predictor.

\subsubsection{Convolutional encoder}
The magnitude spectrogram is treated as a single-channel 2D image $\mathbf{H}^{(0)} \in \mathbb{R}^{1 \times F \times T}$ and processed by a stack of eight 2D convolutional layers, following the VoiceFilter architecture. Each layer is followed by layer normalization and ReLU activation. The layer configurations are:

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}clccl@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Layer} & \textbf{Kernel} (time $\times$ freq) & \textbf{Dilation} (time, freq) & \textbf{Filters} & \textbf{Purpose} \\
\midrule
1 & $(1, 7)$ & $(1, 1)$ & 64 & Frequency-only: local spectral patterns \\
2 & $(7, 1)$ & $(1, 1)$ & 64 & Time-only: temporal dynamics \\
3 & $(5, 5)$ & $(1, 1)$ & 64 & Joint time-frequency \\
4 & $(5, 5)$ & $(2, 1)$ & 64 & Dilated in time \\
5 & $(5, 5)$ & $(4, 1)$ & 64 & Dilated in time \\
6 & $(5, 5)$ & $(8, 1)$ & 64 & Dilated in time \\
7 & $(5, 5)$ & $(16, 1)$ & 64 & Dilated in time \\
8 & $(1, 1)$ & $(1, 1)$ & 8  & Channel bottleneck \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

Several design choices are worth noting. Layers~1--2 are separable-style convolutions: the $(1, 7)$ kernel scans only frequency to detect local spectral patterns (e.g.\ harmonic spacing), while the $(7, 1)$ kernel scans only time. Layers~3--7 use joint $(5, 5)$ kernels with dilation that increases \emph{only along the time axis} (factors $1, 2, 4, 8, 16$), giving a temporal receptive field of 31 frames ($\approx$310\,ms) --- the same as our previous 1D design. Dilation is not applied along frequency because frequency bins are already densely spaced at $\approx$31\,Hz; skipping bins would miss physically meaningful spectral structure. Layer~8 is a $1 \times 1$ bottleneck that compresses from 64 channels to 8, reducing the feature dimension before the LSTM.

Unlike 1D convolutions (which treat all $F$ frequency bins as independent input channels), 2D convolutions share weights across frequency positions. This provides frequency translation equivariance: a harmonic pattern detected at 500\,Hz is recognized by the same filter at 1000\,Hz, without needing to learn separate weights.

The output is $\mathbf{H}^{(8)} \in \mathbb{R}^{B \times 8 \times F \times T}$. To prepare it for the LSTM, we flatten the channel and frequency dimensions at each time step:
\[
    \mathbf{g}_t = \text{flatten}(\mathbf{H}^{(8)}_{:,:,t}) \in \mathbb{R}^{8F}, \qquad t = 1, \dots, T
\]
yielding a sequence $\mathbf{G} \in \mathbb{R}^{T \times 8F}$ (with $8F = 2056$ for $F = 257$).

\subsubsection{Speaker conditioning}
The speaker embedding $\mathbf{e} \in \mathbb{R}^d$ is replicated across all $T$ time frames and concatenated to the flattened CNN output:
\[
    \tilde{\mathbf{H}}_t = \bigl[\mathbf{g}_t \;;\; \mathbf{e}\bigr] \in \mathbb{R}^{8F + d}, \qquad t = 1, \dots, T
\]
This injects speaker identity information at every frame, allowing the subsequent layers to learn a speaker-dependent mask.

\subsubsection{Bidirectional LSTM}
The conditioned sequence $\tilde{\mathbf{H}} \in \mathbb{R}^{T \times (8F+d)}$ is fed into a single-layer bidirectional LSTM (BiLSTM) with hidden size $H = 400$ per direction:
\begin{align}
    \overrightarrow{\mathbf{h}}_t &= \text{LSTM}_{\rightarrow}(\tilde{\mathbf{H}}_t, \overrightarrow{\mathbf{h}}_{t-1}) \in \mathbb{R}^{H} \\
    \overleftarrow{\mathbf{h}}_t &= \text{LSTM}_{\leftarrow}(\tilde{\mathbf{H}}_t, \overleftarrow{\mathbf{h}}_{t+1}) \in \mathbb{R}^{H} \\
    \mathbf{h}_t &= \bigl[\overrightarrow{\mathbf{h}}_t \;;\; \overleftarrow{\mathbf{h}}_t\bigr] \in \mathbb{R}^{2H}
\end{align}

The bidirectional formulation is critical for this task. A unidirectional LSTM can only condition frame~$t$'s mask on past frames $1, \dots, t{-}1$. In contrast, the BiLSTM incorporates both past and future context when predicting each frame's mask. This matters for speech extraction because:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Phonemes are coarticulated: the realization of a sound depends on both its predecessor and successor. Future context helps disambiguate which spectral energy belongs to the target speaker versus the interferer.
    \item Overlap regions are locally ambiguous. A frame in which two speakers' harmonics coincide may only become resolvable given the trajectory of the target speaker's pitch in surrounding frames.
\end{itemize}
Since our task is offline (the full mixture is available before processing), there is no latency constraint that would prohibit bidirectional processing.

\subsubsection{Mask prediction}
Two fully connected layers with ReLU and sigmoid activations map the BiLSTM output to a mask value per time-frequency bin:
\begin{align}
    \mathbf{z}_t &= \text{ReLU}(\mathbf{W}_1 \mathbf{h}_t + \mathbf{b}_1) \in \mathbb{R}^{F} \\
    \mathbf{M}_t &= \sigma(\mathbf{W}_2 \mathbf{z}_t + \mathbf{b}_2) \in [0,1]^{F}
\end{align}
The sigmoid ensures that every mask value lies in $[0,1]$, so the mask can only attenuate (never amplify) energy from the mixture.

\subsection{Training objective}

The network is trained to minimize the mean squared error between the masked mixture spectrogram and the ground-truth clean target spectrogram:
\[
    \mathcal{L} = \frac{1}{FT} \bigl\| \mathbf{M} \odot |\mathbf{X}_{\text{mix}}| - |\mathbf{X}_{\text{target}}| \bigr\|_F^2
\]
where $|\mathbf{X}_{\text{target}}|$ is obtained from the aligned clean target waveform generated during synthetic mixture creation.

\subsubsection{Power-law compressed loss (VoiceFilter)}

Both $|\mathbf{X}_{\text{mix}}|$ and $|\mathbf{X}_{\text{target}}|$ are $F \times T$ matrices where entry $(f,t)$ is the STFT magnitude at frequency bin $f$ and time frame $t$. Our current loss computes MSE directly on these raw magnitudes:
\[
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{raw}} = \frac{1}{FT} \bigl\| \mathbf{M} \odot |\mathbf{X}_{\text{mix}}| - |\mathbf{X}_{\text{target}}| \bigr\|_F^2
\]
The problem is dynamic range. A loud vowel bin might have magnitude $10{,}000$ while a quiet fricative bin has magnitude $1$. Consider a single time frame with two bins:

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lcccc@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Bin} & \textbf{Target} & \textbf{Predicted} & \textbf{Raw error$^2$} & \textbf{Compressed error$^2$} \\
\midrule
Loud vowel     & 10{,}000 & 9{,}000 & $(1000)^2 = 10^6$ & $(100 - 94.9)^2 \approx 26$ \\
Quiet fricative & 1       & 0       & $(1)^2 = 1$         & $(1 - 0)^2 = 1$              \\
\midrule
Ratio (loud/quiet) & & & $1{,}000{,}000 : 1$ & $26 : 1$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

With raw MSE the loud bin's error is $10^6\times$ larger --- the gradient almost entirely says ``fix the loud bin.'' The model has zero incentive to recover the quiet fricative, since getting it completely wrong barely changes the loss. The model finds a ``good enough'' mask for the loud bins and plateaus.

VoiceFilter~\cite{VoiceFilter} fixes this by applying an element-wise power-law compression ($p = 0.3$, following Wilson et al.~\cite{Wilson2018}) to every entry \emph{before} computing MSE:
\[
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{compressed}} = \frac{1}{FT} \bigl\| \bigl(\mathbf{M} \odot |\mathbf{X}_{\text{mix}}|\bigr)^{p} - |\mathbf{X}_{\text{target}}|^{p} \bigr\|_F^2
\]
The power-law compression is a concave function that grows slowly for large values and fast for small ones, compressing a $10{,}000\!:\!1$ dynamic range down significantly. Now quiet bins (fricatives, unvoiced consonants) contribute meaningful gradients, forcing the model to care about the full spectrum rather than just the peaks. This is a likely fix for our observed training plateau.

\subsubsection{Compressed input features}

The compression should also be applied to the \emph{input} of the network, not only the loss. Instead of feeding raw magnitude $|\mathbf{X}_{\text{mix}}|$ into the CNN, we feed the compressed version:
\[
    \text{CNN input} = \bigl(|\mathbf{X}_{\text{mix}}| + \epsilon\bigr)^{p}, \quad p = 0.3, \quad \epsilon = 10^{-8}
\]
The mask $\mathbf{M}$ is still applied to the \emph{raw} magnitude for waveform reconstruction:
\[
    |\hat{\mathbf{X}}_{\text{target}}| = \mathbf{M} \odot |\mathbf{X}_{\text{mix}}|
\]
Why? Raw magnitudes have values spanning several orders of magnitude. The CNN weights must simultaneously handle entries of $10{,}000$ and $0.001$, which makes learning unstable. Compression normalizes the input distribution so the CNN sees a more uniform feature space.

\subsubsection{Diagnostic: oracle mask supervision}

Our normal training pipeline has two steps chained together: (1) predict a mask $\mathbf{M}$, then (2) compute loss in the spectrogram domain through the power-law compression. If training fails, it is unclear which step is the problem --- is the model unable to learn masks at all, or are the spectrogram-domain gradients the issue?

To isolate this, we can bypass the spectrogram loss entirely and train the model to directly predict the \emph{oracle mask}. Recall that the mask is applied by element-wise multiplication:
\[
    |\hat{\mathbf{X}}_{\text{target}}|(f,t) = \mathbf{M}(f,t) \cdot |\mathbf{X}_{\text{mix}}|(f,t)
\]
The oracle mask $\mathbf{M}^*$ is the mask that makes this equation exact --- i.e.\ the one that perfectly recovers the target. Solving for $\mathbf{M}$:
\[
    \mathbf{M}^*(f,t) \cdot |\mathbf{X}_{\text{mix}}|(f,t) = |\mathbf{X}_{\text{target}}|(f,t)
    \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
    \mathbf{M}^*(f,t) = \frac{|\mathbf{X}_{\text{target}}|(f,t)}{|\mathbf{X}_{\text{mix}}|(f,t)}
\]
We clamp to $[0, 1]$ because the network's sigmoid output can only produce values in this range, and add $\epsilon = 10^{-8}$ for numerical stability:
\[
    \mathbf{M}^{*}_{f,t} = \text{clamp}\!\left(\frac{|\mathbf{X}_{\text{target}}|_{f,t}}{|\mathbf{X}_{\text{mix}}|_{f,t} + \epsilon},\; 0,\; 1\right)
\]

As a concrete example, consider three time-frequency bins:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Both speakers active:} mixture magnitude 100, target magnitude 60. Oracle mask: $60/100 = 0.6$, so the mask attenuates to recover the target.
    \item \textbf{Only interferer active:} mixture magnitude 80, target magnitude 0. Oracle mask: $0/80 = 0$, suppressing the bin entirely.
    \item \textbf{Only target active:} mixture magnitude 50, target magnitude 50. Oracle mask: $50/50 = 1$, passing the bin through unchanged.
\end{itemize}

The term ``oracle'' reflects that this mask can only be computed during training, where the clean target spectrogram is available. At inference time the target is unknown --- it is precisely what the network must estimate.

The loss becomes a direct regression on the mask:
\[
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{oracle}} = \frac{1}{FT}\bigl\|\mathbf{M} - \mathbf{M}^{*}\bigr\|_F^2
\]
If the model can overfit this loss (drive it to near zero on a single batch), the architecture is capable of learning --- the problem is in the spectrogram-domain loss and its gradients. If it \emph{cannot} overfit even this, something more fundamental is wrong with the architecture or data.
